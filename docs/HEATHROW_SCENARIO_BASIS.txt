ApronLens: Heathrow-informed scenario design

The reference airport is Heathrow. The rendered airport, stand labels A/B/C, runway 09/27, local circuit, flight identifiers, report timings and incident response duration are fictional teaching geometry. The 1.8 km runway is not a reconstruction of a Heathrow runway. This app practices interpreting reports and changing restrictions; it does not implement Heathrow procedures or establish professional competence.

Occupied arrival stand
Heathrow's Allocation of Aircraft Parking Stands, ASGrOps_OSI_047 v3.0, 28 March 2025, section 3.2.5, distinguishes a holding aircraft assigned to an occupied stand from cancellation of that hold. Sections 3.1 and 3.2 describe stand allocation and communication via the Airport Operations Plan. ApronLens applies the information distinction: a plan to reposition the occupant does not establish availability. TRN103 waits for the explicit stand-planning update, then follows the paved route to Stand B. The app does not reproduce Heathrow's AOP symbols or terminal allocation authority.
https://www.heathrow.com/content/dam/heathrow/web/common/documents/company/team-heathrow/airside/operational-safety-insttructions/ASGrOps_OSI_047_Allocation_of_Aircraft_Parking_Stands_v3.pdf

Wildlife on the runway
Heathrow's Wildlife Hazards and Reporting of Birdstrikes, ASWHM_OSI_077 v3.0, 21 November 2023, sections 4, 6 and 7, assigns wildlife management to Airfield Operations and describes reporting of sightings and strikes. A hazard report includes its location, species or description if known, and activity; flight-crew reports are relayed through ATC. ApronLens now uses a visible flock crossing the runway centreline, a report with those details, an explicitly unconfirmed species and no reported strike. The aircraft holds. Birds disperse, an inspection report arrives, and only then does a separate exercise-controller release permit entry. The specific hold duration, inspection interval and release sequence are authored training choices, not extracted Heathrow timings or an asserted Heathrow SOP.
https://www.heathrow.com/content/dam/heathrow/web/common/documents/company/team-heathrow/airside/operational-safety-insttructions/ASWHM_OSI_077_Wildlife_Hazards_and_Reporting_v3.pdf

UK CAA CAP 772, second edition October 2017, chapters 2, 4 and 6, supports hazard identification, risk assessment, record keeping and distinguishing confirmed, unconfirmed and significant wildlife encounters. The app therefore does not turn a visible bird into a confirmed strike or assume damage. Species and causation stay unconfirmed unless a report establishes them. The planned lesson evaluates source-supported awareness, not wildlife-control methods.
https://www.caa.co.uk/data-and-publications/publications/documents/content/cap-772/
https://www.caa.co.uk/publication/download/13426

Turnaround and departure
Heathrow's Aircraft Arrival, Turnaround and Departure Procedures on Stand, ASGrOps_OSI_093 v1.0, 30 October 2024, defines separate arrival, turnaround and departure phases and requires operator-specific procedures. This supports keeping baggage completion, the off-block estimate, readiness and movement permission separate in the existing baggage-delay exercise. A new estimate does not create a clearance in ApronLens.
https://www.heathrow.com/content/dam/heathrow/web/common/documents/company/team-heathrow/airside/operational-safety-insttructions/ASGrOps_OSI_093_Aircraft_Arrival_Turnaround_and_Departure_on_Stand_v1.pdf

Dropped baggage in the service area
Heathrow's Airside Newsletter, February 2024, pages 2–3, discusses dropped loads on stands, service roads and in baggage areas, including securing loads and baggage-cart curtains. ApronLens uses this as the basis for an authored dropped-baggage incident: a cart approaches a parked aircraft, its rear gate opens and bags fall into the service area. Reports separately establish the obstruction, recovery, inspection, loading completion and release of the ground hold. Collection alone does not release the aircraft. The animation does not establish a real incident's cause, damage, injury or a Heathrow response-time standard. The cart, luggage and articulated gull-like birds are original procedural meshes; their appearance does not identify the reported bird species.
https://www.heathrow.com/content/dam/heathrow/web/common/documents/company/team-heathrow/airside/airside-standards/10596_Heathrow_Airside_Newsletter_Feb24_V3.pdf

Engine fire after parking
The public Heathrow Emergency Orders notice, ASEO_OSI_076 v9.0, 10 July 2026, describes alerting, responsibilities and coordination, and states that the full orders have controlled distribution. ApronLens does not claim to reproduce those orders. Its separate fictional engine-fire event practices following an incident report, keeping an affected aircraft stopped and distinguishing containment from permission to reopen a stand. The earlier birds are not asserted to be its cause. Flame and smoke effects are illustrative, not a physical fire model or response-time standard.
https://www.heathrow.com/content/dam/heathrow/web/common/documents/company/team-heathrow/airside/operational-safety-insttructions/ASEO_OSI_076_Emergency_Orders_v9.pdf

Distinct scenarios and realism
The presentation separates the cases and uses time scaling within them. The core lessons concern operational disruption and evidence interpretation. Dropped loads and wildlife are recognised hazards; aircraft–vehicle contact and stand fire are abnormal events, while a damaging landing is an exceptional emergency example. This is a qualitative teaching distinction, not a measured Heathrow event-frequency model. The current driver authors a shared source timeline for recording; chapter boundaries do not imply that every case was run as an independent acceptance test.

Aircraft–vehicle contact
Heathrow Airside Incident Reporting and Investigation, ASSMS_OSI_067 v3, section 3.3, explicitly includes dropped loads, aircraft/ground-equipment collisions, failures to give way and incursions. Heathrow's safety-alert index also lists vehicle roll/contact on stand (28 October 2025), failure to give way (24 October and 21 November 2025), and ground collision (11 April 2024). ApronLens's service-vehicle contact is an authored case: the aircraft stops, the route is isolated, the vehicle is secured, and aircraft inspection/release remain pending. Cause, damage extent and injury status remain unconfirmed.

Damaging landing and runway closure
The AAIB's AAR 1/2010 concerns G-YMMM at Heathrow on 17 January 2008. It establishes that a serious landing accident is a real hazard category; it does not validate ApronLens's gear-collapse/fire sequence. Our fictional aircraft makes an emergency approach, touches down, decelerates with a damaged-gear illustration and remains stopped. Reports retain the runway closure after fire containment while recovery/inspection are pending. Do not describe this as BA38, a typical arrival, validated flight dynamics or a reconstruction. Containment intervals are compressed teaching timings, not emergency-response standards.
https://www.gov.uk/aaib-reports/1-2010-boeing-777-236er-g-ymmm-17-january-2008

Winter context
Heathrow's current winter-operations page publishes an Airside Snow Plan and de-icing material for 2026/27. Snow, de-icing queues and changing surface conditions are credible seasonal training contexts. This immediate Checkpoint 1 extension shows visual snowfall, rain and late-afternoon-to-night lighting. It does not implement snow accumulation, de-icing queues, braking or operational visibility; those require report-grounded tasks in Checkpoint 2. No snowfall frequency is inferred from the existence of a contingency plan.
https://www.heathrow.com/company/team-heathrow/airside/useful-publications/winter-operations

AI-assisted monitoring at Heathrow
NATS describes testing AI-driven digital-tower technology at Heathrow to address visibility restrictions. Searidge's AIMEE page describes camera-based aircraft segmentation and notifications when an aircraft clears runway thresholds; it frames AI as assistance to human control. These sources motivate ApronLens's planned Checkpoint 2 comparison with and without camera evidence. They do not establish autonomous Heathrow ATC, a deployed language-model controller, or visual-recognition accuracy for ApronLens. The current assistant selects eligible report facts.

Mobile training fit

Heathrow describes assigned controller positions, headsets and stylus-operated touchscreens; this does not establish routine smartphone use for live control. SESAR has studied handheld/tablet multi-touch interfaces, with further safety evaluation identified. ENAC's ELSA supports controller training on computers and tablets. These sources support investigating portable training, not a claim that ApronLens is suitable for operational control or already validated on phones.

Ground-role mobile evidence

RugGear's dnata Singapore case describes rugged smartphones for airside communication, incident reporting, flight/baggage information and checklists. Damarel's FiNDnet Mobile explicitly supports smartphones/tablets for turnaround tracking, service recording and task communication. SITA's 2025 easyJet account documents a mobile passenger-service trial across five UK airports. These are specific deployment/product examples, not a measured ranking of phone-use frequency or proof of current Heathrow adoption. Turnaround coordination is therefore a well-supported candidate for the planned M2 handover task; the delivered M1 controller exercises are not silently relabelled.
