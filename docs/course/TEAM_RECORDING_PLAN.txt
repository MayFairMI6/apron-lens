Suggested narration:

“AirfieldLens helps a learner follow a changing airport situation and work out what the available reports actually establish. We are showing selected excerpts from a recorded native iOS Simulator run. The exercises focus on an obstructed ground route, an arriving aircraft waiting for an occupied stand, and delayed baggage affecting a departure estimate.

The learner starts with a short briefing. During practice, they watch the aircraft, select a flight, read incoming reports and ask the assistant questions. There are three checkpoint moments in each lesson. At each checkpoint, the scene, assistant and history are hidden, and the learner answers a four-choice question. Feedback explains the answer using the reports available at that moment. The lesson ends with a debrief. The scored learner flow is separate from the guided recording used for these movement clips.

Here, TRN103 is waiting for Stand B. The important question is whether the stand is confirmed available. A plan for the occupying aircraft to move is not enough. At this answer's cutoff, the report still says the stand is occupied.

Now a later report confirms availability and releases the exercise hold. The answer changes because the evidence has changed. Selecting the flight gives the question its context, and the cutoff tells us which reports were available to answer it.

Sources opens the reports supporting that particular answer. Recent reports can include updates received afterward. That difference helps the learner check both the evidence and its timing.

The local Qwen model selects relevant report facts. A separate checker checks the claims against eligible facts, and the app displays the accepted summary. The responses in this recording came from actual requests; they were not replaced with rehearsed answers.

Next, we will look at how the moving scenario is driven and what we have evaluated.”

The stand-fire scenario is another emergency example. It is separate from the earlier wildlife sighting; that sighting is not established as its cause. Even after the fire is contained, the stand remains closed pending inspection. The disappearance of flames is not a reopening instruction.

The damaging-landing scenario shows an emergency approach, touchdown, a gear-collapse illustration and a stopped aircraft on the runway. The reports establish the runway closure and response. When the fire is reported contained, recovery and inspection are still pending, so the runway remains closed. This is an exceptional training case, not a typical arrival or a reconstruction of a particular Heathrow accident.

The regional view places Terminal 3, the runways and Wraysbury Reservoir together. We can move from the wider airport to the terminal and read its sign on the building. The scenery combines mapped geography with illustrative building detail.

Four more cases show how a situation can change on or near the runway. These are fictional training examples informed by published incident reports.

First, a rejected takeoff. The aircraft starts its takeoff roll, then brakes to a stop. The crew has reported unreliable airspeed, but the cause is not yet known. The learner checks what has been reported about the aircraft and whether the runway is available. Stopping safely does not mean the runway has reopened.

Next, TRN101 goes around because TRN102 is still on the runway. Watch the approaching aircraft climb away while the other aircraft remains below. The learner identifies the two aircraft and checks the latest runway report. A go-around does not, by itself, mean there has been an accident.

In the vehicle-incursion case, a maintenance vehicle enters the runway. The aircraft stays stationary because its takeoff clearance is cancelled before the roll begins. Watch the vehicle turn and leave. The next question is whether the aircraft has been authorised to depart again; the vehicle leaving is only part of the answer.

Finally, an aircraft makes an emergency water landing at Wraysbury Reservoir. The reservoir is real, but this emergency is fictional. We use it to practise following the reported location and response. The learner can ask what has happened and what is still unknown. A stopped aircraft and a request for help do not tell us that everyone has been evacuated.

The realistic part we are practising is how the learner follows evidence and restrictions. The geometry, response timing and animations are authored. These are visual conditions: they do not yet alter braking, taxi speed, de-icing or reported visibility.

In Checkpoint 2, we plan to turn these conditions into learning tasks. A learner would track an aircraft or ground vehicle as lighting changes, identify when the view no longer supports a confident observation, and use the available reports to explain what remains unconfirmed. Rain and snow exercises would introduce reported surface restrictions or de-icing dependencies; the appearance of weather alone would not establish an operational condition. We would compare assistant answers with and without images to test whether visual evidence helps. In Checkpoint 3, we would evaluate errors and response times across conditions and test whether learners can use the combined view and reports effectively. These are planned tasks; the current visuals have not yet demonstrated a learning benefit.

Driver access for Person 2 — preparation notes, not a passage to read aloud

The video shows the driver's effects: the clock, 30×/normal-speed changes, aircraft paths, incident animation and arriving reports. It does not show a separate driver console. The driver runs on the Mac, independently of the Unity viewer and the language model, and is included in the published repository.

After the repository and local model are set up, run this from the repository folder:
.venv-candidates/bin/python -m backend.training --exercise late_arrival --mode demo --seed 17

Use --mode lesson for three scored checkpoints, or --mode continuous for the hour at normal speed. Run one service at a time on port 8767. Open the configured iOS Simulator app after the model finishes loading. The model and driver stay on the Mac; the simulator is the viewer. A clone alone does not install the model, Unity or Xcode.

Access and setup: https://github.com/MayFairMI6/apron-lens/blob/codex/checkpoint1-controller-training/docs/TRAINING_DRIVER.md
Add --environment rain, --environment snow, or --environment dusk to the launch command for visual conditions. The default remains clear daytime. The driver publishes versioned time-stamped intensity/light frames; TrainingWeather.cs renders them using the shared source clock. The weather preview clips disable question/teaching pauses in a private recording harness; they add no assistant answers or scoring.

Implementation: backend/training_environment.py defines the visual timeline; backend/lessons.py creates the seeded world and reports; backend/training_session.py owns time and lesson state; backend/training.py exposes the service; ExternalTrafficDriver.cs receives it in Unity.

Running or sharing the app

The native iOS Simulator app has been built and tested. Running it again requires both the installed app and the local scenario/model service. The repository's TEAMMATE_SETUP.md and CHECKPOINT1_CURRENT.md explain the Apple Silicon Mac, Unity, Xcode and model setup. Both people can prepare narration from the recording without installing that environment.

AirfieldLens is positioned as portable scenario practice and review for learners, not an operational control workstation. The phone suits a focused flight/report/checkpoint task; a tablet or larger display is the recommended format for a broader traffic overview. This is a design recommendation, not a completed controller-usability finding. Checkpoint 3 should compare phone and larger-screen use with representative learners. ENAC’s ELSA is a relevant computer/tablet training precedent: https://www.enac.fr/en/elsa-training-differently-training-anywhere

No TestFlight build has been published. The current service listens only on the original Mac's loopback address. A phone-distributable build would need device signing and an accessible authenticated service, followed by App Store Connect/TestFlight setup. For this video, the shared recording is the immediately usable route. Simulator verification does not establish the assignment's physical-smartphone requirement.
