Suggested narration

“The external scenario service owns the clock, aircraft paths, incidents and report arrivals. The language model answers questions about those reports; it does not control the aircraft. A fixed seed makes the scenario repeatable.

We present these as distinct training scenarios, with time scaling within each case. Quiet periods can run at thirty times normal speed. Teaching moments and pending answers run at normal speed, with time afterward to read the response. The scene clock and each answer’s cutoff remain visible. These selected recordings are examples, not a claim that all these incidents are likely in one hour.

In the wildlife scenario, birds cross the runway area while the aircraft holds short. The answer comes from the wildlife report and the pending inspection. Seeing the birds leave does not establish a movement release. The separate update does. The flight movement footage then shows line-up, takeoff, climb, return approach, touchdown and a curved runway exit. We can use the apron, runway or follow-flight view.

The dropped-baggage scenario shows bags falling from a cart beside a parked aircraft. The first reports establish the obstruction and hold. Later the bags have been collected, but inspection and movement release are still pending at the answer’s cutoff. Those arrive separately. Visible cleanup, service completion and permission to move are different facts.

The aircraft–vehicle contact scenario asks a different question: what restriction remains after an incident? TRN102 and the service vehicle stop at the contact point. The assistant identifies the reported contact and hold. A later report says the vehicle is secured, but the aircraft still requires inspection and a movement release. We have not established the cause, damage extent or injury status.

The stand-fire scenario is another emergency example. It is separate from the earlier wildlife sighting; that sighting is not established as its cause. Even after the fire is contained, the stand remains closed pending inspection. The disappearance of flames is not a reopening instruction.

Finally, the damaging-landing scenario shows an emergency approach, touchdown, a gear-collapse illustration and a stopped aircraft on the runway. The reports establish the runway closure and response. When the fire is reported contained, recovery and inspection are still pending, so the runway remains closed. This is an exceptional training case, not a typical arrival or a reconstruction of a particular Heathrow accident.

The realistic part we are practising is how the learner follows evidence and restrictions. The geometry, response timing and animations are authored. Heathrow’s own publications describe occupied stands, dropped loads, wildlife and aircraft–vehicle hazards. The following environment previews add rain, snowfall, and daylight changing through dusk into night. The external driver supplies these timelines, and the viewer renders them against the same source clock as the aircraft and reports. They accelerate and pause with that clock. These captures run at thirty times normal speed to make the changes visible. Rain and snow are separate settings; the dusk preview spans one source hour, not a full twenty-four-hour day. These are visual conditions: they do not yet alter braking, taxi speed, de-icing or reported visibility. Weather-related decisions belong to Checkpoint 2.

Guided demonstration runs the scene and assistant questions without asking the viewer to choose answers or awarding points. The separate practice lessons have three scored checkpoints and report-based feedback. All three short lessons have reached debrief in the native iOS Simulator. The four new collision and landing answers came from real local-model requests and passed the evidence checks. That verifies these examples, not general model accuracy. The new incident recordings are targeted captures, not another uninterrupted full-hour acceptance run.

Our earlier frozen Milestone 1 study used forty distinct question-and-answer turns. Under its full-context condition, Qwen3.5-9B scored twenty-four out of forty; Qwen3-VL-4B and the deterministic baseline each scored sixteen. Images were off, and tracking context did not improve scores in that setup. These results describe the earlier evaluation configuration.

There is a relevant real-world direction: NATS and Searidge describe AI-assisted digital-tower trials at Heathrow, using camera evidence to help monitor aircraft and runway exits. That supports the motivation for our next checkpoint. ApronLens currently answers from reports; we have not yet demonstrated reliable AI interpretation of the camera view.

Checkpoint 2 will evaluate questions that require visible evidence, compare answers with and without images, develop a distinct turnaround handover task, and add report-grounded weather decisions such as de-icing dependencies and surface restrictions. Checkpoint 3 will evaluate the integrated experience, including latency and failures. Physical-phone verification remains outstanding.”
